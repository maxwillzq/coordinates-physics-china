# Part 3: The Quantum Revolution and the Pioneers of "Flesh-and-Blood Interfacing" (1900–1949)

> *"A university is not defined by large buildings, but by big masters."* —— Mei Yiqi

![Inverted Image in Ink Wash Style](/img/part3_ink_wash.png)

## World Axis
The first half of the 20th century was the most exciting era in the history of physics. Starting from 1900 when Planck proposed the energy quantum hypothesis, the dark clouds of classical physics triggered a disruptive "Quantum Revolution." Subsequently, Einstein proposed the theory of relativity, completely reshaping the view of space and time; and a large number of physicists such as Bohr, Heisenberg, Schrödinger, and Dirac constructed the magnificent theoretical edifice of quantum mechanics in just two or three decades. Physics moved from macro to micro, and from determinism to probability theory, and the paradigm underwent a fundamental shift. Physics in this period not only changed human cognition of nature but also laid the theoretical foundation for the later information technology and nuclear energy era.

## China Point
While world physics was undergoing a violent revolution, China was experiencing the most severe social turbulence and war baptism in modern history. However, it was exactly this batch of pioneers called "flesh-and-blood interfacing" who, under the extreme predicament of a poor and weak country and continuous wars, arduously initiated the institutionalization process of modern physics in China. The first batch of Gengzi indemnity students represented by Hu Gangfu, Rao Yutai, Ye Qisun, and Wu Youxun (the "Four Great Mentors" of Chinese physics),resolutely returned to China after completing their studies, established physics departments in universities like Tsinghua and Peking University, and trained a whole generation of outstanding scientists including Qian Sanqiang, Deng Jiaxian, Yang Chen-Ning, and Tsung-Dao Lee. Especially in the Southwest Associated University during the Anti-Japanese War, Chinese physicists maintained world-class teaching and research levels in thatched huts, writing a miracle in the history of human education and science.

## Coordinate Value
From the perspective of the coordinate system of "World Axis - China Point," Chinese physics in this period was in the stage of "catching up and laying foundations." Faced with the wildly advancing quantum revolution in the West, the strategy of Chinese physicists was "flesh-and-blood interfacing"—using their blood and flesh and the responsibility of intellectuals to build the discipline edifice of modern physics on ruins. They not only brought back the most cutting-edge scientific knowledge but also established a rigorous scientific spirit and an independent research tradition. From the perspective of the philosophy of science, this was not only a discipline catch-up but also a flesh-and-blood answer to the "Needham Question." While Western physicists were debating the essence of the microscopic world in the storm of quantum paradigm shift, the first generation of Chinese physicists faced the "double predicament of catch-up players": they had to make up for the lessons of classical physics and simultaneously keep up with the latest frontier of quantum mechanics, while also establishing the education and research institutions of modern science in the war. Although there was still a gap in the absolute height of pure theoretical innovation compared with the world's top level, Chinese physics completed the discipline institutionalization from scratch in such a short time and trained scientific giants who would shine in the world in the future. Its coordinate value lies in demonstrating the great practice of cultural consciousness and saving the nation through science.

## Content
### Chapter 1: The Quantum Storm and the Overthrow of the Classical Paradigm
At the end of the 19th century, the edifice of physics seemed to be basically completed. Lord Kelvin declared in his 1900 speech that the beautiful clear sky of physics had only "two clouds" left: one was the null result of the Michelson-Morley experiment, and the other was the "ultraviolet catastrophe" in the blackbody radiation experiment. However, it was exactly these two clouds that ultimately triggered a storm sweeping through the entire physics, completely overturning the dominant position of classical physics.

#### Planck's Shocking Leap: The Birth of Energy Quantum
On December 14, 1900, at the meeting of the German Physical Society, Max Planck proposed a disruptive hypothesis. To solve the problem of the blackbody radiation formula diverging in the short-wave band, he was forced to make an assumption violating the principle of classical physics that "nature does not make jumps": energy is not continuous when emitted and absorbed but is carried out in minimal, indivisible units. Planck called this unit "Energy Quanta," and its size is proportional to the radiation frequency, i.e., $E = h\nu$, where $h$ was later called the Planck constant.

This day is recognized as the birthday of quantum mechanics. Planck himself was initially very uneasy about this assumption; he thought it was just a mathematical coincidence and tried to find an explanation within the framework of classical physics. However, the "ghost" he released would soon completely change the face of physics.

#### Einstein's Miracle Year: Light Quanta and Relativity
In 1905, Albert Einstein, working in the patent office in Bern, Switzerland, published five papers that changed the world. In the paper on the photoelectric effect, Einstein extended Planck's concept of energy quantum to light itself, proposing the concept of "Photon" (Light Quanta). He pointed out that light is not only quantized when emitted and absorbed but is also composed of discrete energy particles in the process of propagation. This hypothesis perfectly explained the photoelectric effect and was confirmed more than a decade later by experiments of Millikan, Wu Youxun, and others. Einstein was also awarded the 1921 Nobel Prize in Physics for this.

In the same year, Einstein also published a paper on special relativity, completely overturning Newton's absolute view of space and time. He proposed the principle of constant light speed and the principle of special relativity, pointing out that time and space are relative and are interweaving "spacetime." The birth of relativity, together with quantum theory, constituted the two pillars of modern physics.

#### Bohr's Atomic Model: Stationary States and Transitions
In 1913, Niels Bohr, a young Danish physicist, introduced the quantum concept into the atomic structure. To solve the problem of the instability of Rutherford's nuclear atom model under classical electromagnetic theory, Bohr proposed three revolutionary hypotheses:
1. Electrons in an atom can only move on specific, discrete orbits, which are called "stationary states," and electrons do not radiate energy when moving on stationary states.
2. When electrons transition between different stationary states, they will absorb or emit photons of specific frequencies, and the energy difference satisfies $\Delta E = h\nu$.
3. The angular momentum of electron orbits is also quantized.

The Bohr model successfully explained the laws of the hydrogen atom spectrum, pushing quantum theory to a new height. Bohr also became the "godfather" of quantum theory, and his Copenhagen Institute became a holy place for young physicists all over the world.

#### Establishment of Quantum Mechanics: Matrices, Waves, and Uncertainty
From 1925 to 1927, quantum theory ushered in its golden explosion period, ultimately evolving into a mature quantum mechanics theory.

In 1925, Bohr's assistant Werner Heisenberg abandoned the concept of unobservable electron orbits and started only from observable physical quantities (such as spectral frequency and intensity) to create "Matrix Mechanics." This was the first complete form of quantum mechanics, but because it used matrix mathematics unfamiliar to physicists at that time, it appeared obscure and difficult to understand.

In 1926, Erwin Schrödinger, an Austrian physicist, started from de Broglie's matter wave hypothesis and created "Wave Mechanics" from another perspective. He proposed the famous Schrödinger equation, using wave functions to describe the state of microscopic particles. Wave mechanics was more in line with physicists' habits in mathematics, thus obtaining wider applications.

Soon, Schrödinger and Dirac proved that matrix mechanics and wave mechanics are completely equivalent in mathematics. Subsequently, Max Born gave a statistical interpretation of wave functions, pointing out that the square of the wave function represents the probability of a particle appearing somewhere. This interpretation completely broke the determinism of classical mechanics and introduced probability theory into the bottom layer of physics.

In 1927, Heisenberg proposed the famous "Uncertainty Principle," pointing out that the position and momentum of a microscopic particle cannot be measured accurately at the same time, and their product is greater than or equal to the Planck constant divided by $4\pi$. This principle shows that the fuzziness of the microscopic world is not due to the imperfection of measuring instruments but is an essential property of nature.

#### Copenhagen Interpretation and the Debate of Solvay Conference
The "Copenhagen School" represented by Bohr and Heisenberg gradually formed a standard interpretation of quantum mechanics, mainly including: Born's probability interpretation, Heisenberg's uncertainty principle, and Bohr's complementarity principle (wave-particle duality).

However, this interpretation overturning classical determinism was strongly opposed by Einstein, Schrödinger, and others. Einstein firmly believed that "God does not play dice"; he believed that quantum mechanics, though effective, was not complete, and there should be a deeper hidden variable determinism behind it.

The fifth Solvay Conference in 1927 became the peak of this debate. The world's top physicists gathered together; Einstein continuously proposed ingenious "thought experiments" to refute quantum mechanics, while Bohr could cleverly resolve them every time. Although this debate did not finalize a winner, it greatly promoted people's understanding of the essence of quantum mechanics.

### Chapter 2: Gengzi Indemnity Students and the Institutionalization of Chinese Physics
While world physics was experiencing a quantum storm, China was experiencing deep suffering from internal and external troubles. However, it was exactly under such extreme predicament that a batch of intellectuals harboring the ideal of saving the nation through science arduously initiated the institutionalization process of modern physics in China. They were vividly called the pioneers of "flesh-and-blood interfacing"—using their own blood and flesh to build a bridge between Eastern and Western science.

#### Gengzi Indemnity and the First Batch of Pioneers
The starting point of modern physics in China has an inseparable connection with the "Gengzi Indemnity." In 1908, the US Congress passed a bill deciding to return part of the Gengzi indemnity to fund Chinese students studying in the US. Subsequently, Britain, France, and other countries also followed suit. Although this policy carried the color of cultural colonization, it objectively trained a large number of top scientific talents for China.

Among this batch of students emerged Hu Gangfu, Rao Yutai, Ye Qisun, and Wu Youxun, who were later called the "Four Great Mentors" of Chinese physics. They received strict scientific training in world-class laboratories in the US and Europe, personally witnessed the explosion of the quantum revolution, and deeply knew the importance of modern science to the country's prosperity.

#### Four Great Mentors and Discipline Institutionalization
**Hu Gangfu** was an outstanding one among the first batch of Gengzi indemnity students. He received a doctorate degree from Harvard University, studying under the famous physicist Bridgman. After returning to China, he successively established physics departments in Nanjing Higher Normal School (later Southeast University, Central University) and Zhejiang University. Hu Gangfu not only brought back the most cutting-edge X-ray spectroscopy knowledge but also, more importantly, personally built laboratories, creating the precedent of experimental physics in China. With an extremely rigorous teaching style, he trained the first batch of native physicists like Wu Youxun and Yan Jici.

**Rao Yutai** received a doctorate degree from Princeton University, studying under Nobel Prize winner Richardson. After returning to China, he founded the Physics Department of Nankai University and chaired the Physics Department of Peking University for a long time. Rao Yutai's research area was spectroscopy, and he did important work in gas discharge and molecular spectra. With profound academic attainments and a humble character, he attracted many excellent young people to join physics.

**Ye Qisun** was a Harvard University PhD, and his mentor was also Bridgman. After returning to China, he chaired the Physics Department and the School of Science of Tsinghua University, and was recognized as the "general designer" of modern physics in China. Ye Qisun not only did world-class work in the precise determination of the Planck constant but also demonstrated superb education and management talents. With the principle of "preferring lack to excess," he selected teachers and laid an extremely high academic starting point for the Tsinghua Physics Department. The list of students he trained was almost half the history of modern Chinese science: Qian Sanqiang, Deng Jiaxian, Yang Chen-Ning, Tsung-Dao Lee, Wang Ganchang, Zhao Jiuzhang...

**Wu Youxun** studied under Compton at the University of Chicago. His most famous achievement was the extremely precise experimental verification of the "Compton Effect." At that time, there was still debate in academic circles about the effect. Wu Youxun used 15 different scattering materials ranging from the light element lithium to the heavy element lead, proving that the wavelength change $\Delta \lambda = \lambda' - \lambda = \frac{h}{m_e c} (1 - \cos \theta)$ was only related to the scattering angle $\theta$, and had nothing to do with the scattering substance. His experiment covered scattering angles from $45^\circ$ to $135^\circ$ and was the most comprehensive and decisive experimental verification of the Compton effect. Compton specially mentioned Wu Youxun's contribution in his speech when receiving the Nobel Prize in 1927 and included Wu Youxun's data charts in his work *X-Rays in Theory and Experiment*. Some even believed that the effect should be called the "Compton-Wu Youxun Effect." After returning to China, Wu Youxun chaired physics departments in Tsinghua University and Central University successively, training a large number of experimental physics talents.

Besides the four great mentors themselves, the first batch of scholars they trained and influenced also began to cut a striking figure on the international stage. **Zhao Zhongyao** was an outstanding representative among them. He graduated from Southeast University in 1925 (studying under Hu Gangfu) and then taught at Tsinghua University. In 1927, he went to California Institute of Technology for further study, studying under Millikan. In 1930, in a hard $\gamma$-ray scattering experiment, Zhao Zhongyao first observed the annihilation radiation of electron-positron pairs and published papers continuously in *PNAS* and *Physical Review*. This was actually the first time humans observed signs of positrons, even earlier than Carl Anderson's discovery of positrons. At that time, Millikan was skeptical of the "anomalous absorption" found by Zhao Zhongyao, but Zhao Zhongyao insisted on his experimental results. Anderson later admitted that it was Zhao Zhongyao's experiment that inspired him, enabling him to ultimately find positrons in cosmic rays. Although due to various reasons, Zhao Zhongyao missed the Nobel Prize, his work was the experimental result closest to the Nobel Prize level achieved by Chinese physicists at the world frontier in that period, making indelible contributions to the establishment of quantum electrodynamics.

Under the leadership of the "Four Great Mentors," the Chinese Physical Society was officially established in 1932. Subsequently, physics departments of various major universities, the Meteorology Department and Geology Department of Tsinghua University were established successively, and the predecessors of the Chinese Academy of Sciences—the Institute of Physics of Academia Sinica and the Institute of Physics of National Academy of Peiping were established successively. So far, Chinese physics completed the discipline institutionalization from scratch.

#### The Madame Curie of the East: The Extraordinary Years of Wu Chien-shiung
Among this batch of "flesh-and-blood interfacing" pioneers and their disciples, **Wu Chien-shiung** must occupy a special page. She entered the Physics Department of Central University in 1930 (studying under Hu Gangfu and others) and went to study in the US in 1936. Wu Chien-shiung was hailed as the "First Lady of Physics" and the "Madame Curie of the East."

Although her most brilliant achievements were achieved in the 1950s, her academic foundation was deeply rooted in the soil where modern physics in China was founded. Her precision in nuclear physics experiments was world-famous. In 1956, when Yang Chen-Ning and Tsung-Dao Lee proposed the hypothesis of parity non-conservation in weak interactions, it was exactly Wu Chien-shiung who, against huge pressure and difficulties, conducted the famous Cobalt-60 decay experiment using extremely low-temperature technology at the end of 1956, successfully confirming this hypothesis. Her classic paper *Experimental Test of Parity Conservation in Beta Decay* was published in early 1957 (Phys. Rev. 105, 1413), shocking the world physics community. Although she failed to receive the Nobel Prize with Yang and Lee, her contribution enjoys a supreme position in the history of physics.

#### The Scientific Miracle of Southwest Associated University
In 1937, the Anti-Japanese War broke out comprehensively, and universities in North China and coastal areas were forced to move inland. Peking University, Tsinghua University, and Nankai University formed the National Southwest Associated University (Southwest Associated University for short) in Kunming.

In the harsh environment of continuous wars and extreme shortage of materials, the Physics Department of Southwest Associated University created a miracle in the history of human education. The classrooms at that time were thatched huts, and teachers and students often had to teach in the intervals of avoiding bombings. Experimental instruments were extremely scarce; physicists used scrap metal to make them by hand.

However, it was exactly in such "thatched huts" that masters like Ye Qisun, Wu Youxun, Rao Yutai, Zhou Peiyuan, Wu Ta-You, and Wang Zhuxi gathered together, maintaining world-class teaching levels. They not only taught the latest quantum mechanics and relativity but also guided students to pay attention to the world's most cutting-edge research.

The Physics Department of Southwest Associated University trained two Nobel Prize winners, Yang Chen-Ning and Tsung-Dao Lee, as well as a large number of "Two Bombs, One Satellite" founding fathers such as Deng Jiaxian, Zhu Guangya, and Zhao Jiuzhang who later made decisive contributions to China's nuclear weapons and space undertakings.

**Yang Chen-Ning** and **Tsung-Dao Lee** were the most outstanding representatives trained by Southwest Associated University. Yang Chen-Ning studied under Wu Ta-You and Wang Zhuxi here, and Tsung-Dao Lee demonstrated astonishing talent under the guidance of Wu Ta-You. They laid a solid theoretical foundation in Southwest Associated University, later went to the US for further study, and jointly published the epoch-making paper *Question of Parity Conservation in Weak Interactions* (Phys. Rev. 104, 254) in 1956. In this paper, they boldly questioned the creed of parity conservation in weak interactions. Although this work was published in 1956 (belonging to the fourth part on the timeline of this book), its seed was sown in the thatched huts of Southwest Associated University. In 1957, they were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics for this, which was also the first time Chinese received the Nobel Prize, greatly encouraging the confidence of Chinese all over the world. Yang Chen-Ning and Tsung-Dao Lee not only reached the peak in theory but also became hopeful lighthouses for Chinese physics moving from "catch-up" to "leading."

Such scientific creativity exploding under extreme predicament became an eternal monument of the spirit of Chinese intellectuals.

#### The Deep Meaning of "Flesh-and-Blood Interfacing"
Looking back at this history, we can better understand the heaviness and greatness of the concept of "flesh-and-blood interfacing."

First, this was the **interfacing of knowledge**. This batch of physicists not only brought back Western scientific knowledge but also brought back rigorous experimental traditions and free academic spirits. They enabled China to leap from knowing nothing about modern physics to being able to understand and participate in the discussion of the quantum revolution in just twenty years.

Second, this was the **interfacing of institutions**. They built physics departments, research institutes, and academic groups from scratch on land without any modern scientific foundation, establishing the social institutionalization of modern science in China.

Most importantly, this was the **responsibility of flesh and blood**. Faced with superior research conditions and personal academic prospects in the West, they resolutely chose to return to the war-torn, poor, and weak motherland. Many of them, for teaching and administrative affairs, had to give up their own personal academic research. They used their own blood and flesh to pave the way for the future of Chinese science. They were true pioneers and the backbone of modern science in China.

### 【Scientific Thinking and Education Revelations】

This part records the start and brilliance of Chinese physics in the most difficult period, providing us with valuable scientific thinking and education revelations:

1. **"Pure Scientific Spirit" in Extreme Environments**: The thatched huts of Southwest Associated University witnessed that the decisive factor of scientific research and high-quality education is not superior material conditions, but the pure pursuit of truth and a rigorous academic attitude. In today's background of greatly enriched material conditions, this "purity" is especially precious.
2. **The Importance of "Institutionalization" and "Mentorship Chain"**: The achievements of Hu Gangfu, Ye Qisun, and others lay not only in personal research but also in that they established China's physics education and research system, and formed a clear chain of mentorship (from the four great mentors to the founding fathers of Two Bombs, One Satellite, and then to Yang and Lee). This reveals to us that the cultivation of top-notch innovative talents requires fertile soil and a continuous ecology, rather than isolated individuals.
3. **The "Double-Wheel Drive" of Experiment and Theory**: Wu Youxun's verification of the Compton effect and Zhao Zhongyao's observation of positron annihilation both demonstrated the extremely high experimental talent of the first generation of Chinese physicists. This reminds us that in current science education, we must overcome the tendency of "emphasizing theory and neglecting experiment"; only solid experimental skills can make decisive contributions at the moment of paradigm shift.
