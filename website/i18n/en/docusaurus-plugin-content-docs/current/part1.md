# Part 1: Parallel Spacetimes and Missed Paradigms (Before the 17th Century)

> *"The shadow: The light shines on a person like an arrow. The lower part of the person appears high, and the higher part appears low."* —— *Mozi, Jing Xia*

![Inverted Image in Ink Wash Style](/img/part1_ink_wash.png)

## World Axis
Before the 17th century, the development of Western physics (or more accurately, natural philosophy) was centered on the system of Aristotle (384 BC – 322 BC). Aristotle's *Physics* was not a quantitative science in the modern sense, but a qualitative philosophy about natural change, motion, and existence. He constructed a grand model of the universe: the universe is finite, spherical, and centered on the Earth. The world below the moon is composed of four elements: water, fire, air, and earth, each having its "natural place," and motion is the tendency of things to find their natural place (e.g., heavy objects fall, light objects rise). The world above the moon is composed of the perfect fifth element, "ether," and undergoes eternal circular motion.

Aristotle's physics was teleological; he believed that all changes in nature had a purpose. He proposed the "Four Causes": material cause, formal cause, efficient cause, and final cause. When explaining object motion, he emphasized that a continuous push was required to maintain motion, leading to complex explanations of "forced motion." This system dominated Western thought for over a thousand years, perfectly merging with Christian theology to become the core of medieval scholasticism.

However, tensions existed within the Aristotelian system. In the 16th century, Copernicus proposed the heliocentric theory, fundamentally challenging the cosmological basis of Aristotle. Subsequently, Tycho's precise astronomical observations, Kepler's three laws of planetary motion, and Galileo's discoveries using the telescope step-by-step disintegrated Aristotle's perfect crystal-sphere universe. Galileo's research on free fall and projectile motion began to introduce quantitative experiments and mathematical descriptions, unveiling the prelude to the modern scientific revolution. The "World Axis" of this period was undergoing a shocking turn from ancient Greek qualitative natural philosophy to modern quantitative experimental科学的惊人转向。

## China Point
Meanwhile, China in ancient times developed extremely rich empirical physical knowledge, demonstrating a unique pragmatism and organic view of nature.

In mechanics and optics, *Mo Jing* (compiled during the Warring States period, around the 4th century BC; Mozi lived around 470 BC – 391 BC) is a shining pearl. *Mo Jing* contains a large number of discussions on mechanics, optics, and geometry, and its scientific nature was rare on a global scale in the same era. For example, its definition of force is "Force, that by which the shape is made to strive" (force is the cause of change in the state of motion of an object), which has a subtle fit with the connotation of Newton's first law two thousand years later. In optics, *Mo Jing* detailedly recorded the straight-line propagation of light, pinhole imaging (formation of inverted images), and reflection imaging laws of plane mirrors, concave mirrors, and convex mirrors, with a level of detail that was breathtaking.

In magnetism and acoustics, ancient China's achievements were even more leading in the world. The invention and application of the compass was a major contribution of China to world civilization. As early as the Warring States period, there were records of "Si Nan" (south-pointer). By the Song Dynasty, Shen Kuo in *Dream Pool Essays* not only recorded the method of making a compass but also first discovered "magnetic declination" (the needle does not point absolutely due south but has a slight declination), which was four hundred years earlier than in the West. In acoustics, ancient China conducted in-depth research on musical temperament; the Zenghouyi chime bells of the Warring States period demonstrated superb casting technology and a profound understanding of resonance and double tones.

However, these excellent pieces of knowledge were mostly scattered in various classics, technical notes, or occult works, and failed to form a logically rigorous and complete theoretical framework like Aristotelian physics. Ancient China's view of nature leaned more toward organic naturalism and holism, emphasizing Yin and Yang, the Five Elements, Qi theory, and the "unity of heaven and man." Under this paradigm, nature was viewed as an interconnected organic whole rather than a mechanical clock that could be disassembled and studied.

## Coordinate Value
From the coordinate system of "World Axis - China Point," the development of Chinese and Western physics before the 17th century was in a state of "parallel spacetimes."

China was in a leading position in many specific empirical discoveries and technological inventions, such as the application of magnetism and empirical observation in optics. However, the Chinese cultural tradition lacked the formal logic and axiomatic mathematics of ancient Greece (represented by Euclid's *Elements*). Ancient Greeks were keen to start from a few axioms and draw conclusions through strict logical reasoning, building a solid theoretical edifice. Ancient Chinese mathematics leaned more toward practical algorithms, such as *The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art*, solving specific problems like land measurement and taxation, lacking the tradition of abstract logical proof.

This prevented China's empirical knowledge from being transformed into modern systematic experimental science. China's scientific exploration was driven more by practical needs (such as calendar, metallurgy, navigation) rather than by pure curiosity and the mathematized pursuit of the ultimate laws of the universe.

Therefore, China missed the birth of the modern physics paradigm, not because of a lack of intelligence among the Chinese people, but because its unique cultural and ideological paradigm led to a different development path. When the West began to combine mathematics and experiment, moving toward the reductionism and mechanism of modern science, China continued to run smoothly on the track of organicism and empiricism. This "parallelism" eventually led to a huge "parallax" after the 17th century.

## Content
### Chapter 1: Ancient Physical Wisdom and Empirical Accumulation

#### Section 1: The Dawn of Physics in "Mo Jing"
*Mo Jing* is a collection of works of the Mohist school during the Warring States period, containing rich natural science knowledge, especially in mechanics and optics, and can be called the pinnacle of ancient Chinese physics.

In optics, *Mo Jing* contains eight records about optics, systematically discussing the relationship between light and shadow, pinhole imaging, and the imaging laws of plane mirrors, concave mirrors, and convex mirrors.
1. **Straight-line propagation of light and pinhole imaging**: *Mo Jing* states: "The light of a person shines like an arrow. The lower part of the person appears high, and the higher part appears low. The feet block the lower light, so an image is formed above; the head blocks the upper light, so an image is formed below. Because there is a point (aperture) far and near with the light, the image is projected inside." This passage extremely accurately explains the principle of pinhole imaging. It points out that light propagates in straight lines like shooting arrows, and when passing through a small hole (point), due to the intersection of light rays, the light from the upper part of the object shines below, and the light from the lower part shines above, thus forming an inverted image on the barrier. This discovery was deeper and more accurate than Aristotle's observations on similar phenomena in *Problems* in the West. This is starkly different from Aristotle's qualitative view in *On the Soul* which viewed light as a certain state (i.e., 'actuality') of a transparent medium. Mozi viewed light as directional 'rays' (shines like an arrow), and this intuition was closer to the physical substance of modern geometric optics.
2. **Reflection Optics**: *Mo Jing* also studied specular reflection. For plane mirrors, it pointed out that the image and the object are equal in size and reversed left to right. For concave mirrors and convex mirrors, Mohist scholars had observed the existence of a focus and distinguished different imaging situations when the object distance is within or beyond the focus (erect magnified, inverted reduced, etc.). Such qualitative empirical observation was unparalleled at that time.

In mechanics, *Mo Jing* also had profound insights.
1. **Definition of Force**: "Force, that by which the weight is called down" or "Force, that by which the shape is made to strive." The latter links force with the change of the state of motion of an object, believing that force is the cause of an object moving from rest or changing its direction of motion. Although this did not reach the quantitative height of Newton's second law, it had conceptually surpassed Aristotle's view that "motion must be maintained by a push," forming a sharp contrast. Mozi's definition of force actually touched the edge of the law of inertia, that is, force is the cause of changing the state of motion, not the cause of maintaining motion.
2. **Lever Principle**: *Mo Jing* discussed the balance conditions of weighing instruments (scales and levers), pointing out the relationship among "ben" (fulcrum), "biao" (lever arm), and "quan" (weight). Although no precise mathematical formula was given, the description of the balance conditions already contained the concept of torque.

#### Section 2: The Scientific Peak of the Song Dynasty and "Dream Pool Essays"
By the Song Dynasty, ancient Chinese science and technology reached another peak, and Shen Kuo's (1031–1095) *Dream Pool Essays* (around 1088) was the comprehensive work of this period. Shen Kuo was called "the coordinate in the history of Chinese science" by Joseph Needham.

1. **Discovery of Magnetic Declination**: *Dream Pool Essays* records: "Magicians rub a needle with a magnet, then it can point south, but it always deviates slightly to the east, not completely south." This is the world's earliest record of magnetic declination. Shen Kuo not only recorded this phenomenon but also experimented with four different compass suspension methods (water floating, fingernail, bowl rim, and silk suspension), pointing out that the "silk suspension method" (hanging by a single silk thread stuck to the center of the needle) was the most sensitive. This discovery was of decisive significance for navigation and was hundreds of years earlier than the record in Amalfi, Europe.
2. **Further Exploration in Optics**: Shen Kuo conducted more in-depth experimental research on concave mirror imaging. He moved his finger in front of a concave mirror, observed the change of the image, and introduced the concept of "ai" (i.e., focus), explaining why the image becomes inverted beyond the "ai".
3. **Acoustic Resonance**: Shen Kuo also recorded experiments on sound resonance ("void can accommodate sound"). He cut a paper man and put it on a zither string; when playing the corresponding string of another zither, the paper man would jump, vividly demonstrating the resonance of sound waves.

#### Section 3: The Systematizing Power of Western Aristotelian Physics
Compared with the empirical wisdom shining in China's *Mo Jing* and *Dream Pool Essays*, Western physics before the 17th century was based on Aristotle's philosophical system.

Aristotle's physics was centered on its systematicness and logical completeness.
1. **Four Elements and Natural Place**: He believed that everything on earth is composed of four elements: earth, water, air, and fire. Earth is the heaviest, and its natural place is at the center of the universe; water is second; air is third; fire is the lightest, and its natural place is below the lunar orbit. The motion of an object is to return to its natural place. Although this explanation seems wrong in modern times, it provided a unified framework that could explain almost all ground phenomena observed at that time.
2. **Teleological Universe**: Aristotle believed that there is no accident in nature, and everything has a purpose. Heavy objects fall because they long to return to the embrace of the earth. This teleological view of nature perfectly fit with medieval Christian theology, and God was regarded as the Ultimate Cause of the universe.
3. **Lack of Mathematization**: A fatal weakness of Aristotelian physics was its exclusion of mathematics. He believed that mathematics deals with abstract, unchanging concepts, while nature is constantly changing, so mathematics cannot be applied to physics. This was diametrically opposed to Archimedes' later attempt at mathematization in statics and the path of the modern scientific revolution.

To summarize this chapter, we can see that ancient China made eye-catching empirical achievements in various branches of physics (force, light, sound, magnetism), and its keen observation and ingenious experiments were unique in the world. However, this knowledge failed to aggregate into a rigorous theoretical system. While the West was not necessarily leading in specific empirical discoveries, its inherited ancient Greek rationalist tradition and Aristotle's systematized thinking provided a target that could be criticized and surpassed for the later scientific revolution.

### Chapter 2: Differences in Paradigms and Parallel Development

#### Section 1: The Fundamental Divide in Thinking Methods: Logic and Intuition
The parallel development of Chinese and Western physics before the 17th century was rooted in the deep differences in thinking methods and philosophical paradigms behind them.

1. **The West: Formal Logic and Axiomatic Tradition**
Since ancient Greece, Western thinking has been deeply influenced by formal logic. Aristotle's logic (syllogism) provided a rigorous tool for rational thinking. In the scientific field, Euclid's *Elements* set a model for axiomatic systems—starting from a few obvious axioms and deriving hundreds of theorems through strict logical reasoning. This thinking method requires clear concepts, rigorous reasoning, and self-consistency. When this tradition is combined with physics, it inevitably moves toward an axiomatic physics seeking universal laws.

2. **China: Dialectical Thinking and Relational Cognition**
In contrast, ancient Chinese thinking leaned more toward dialectical and relational thinking. The theory of Yin and Yang and the Five Elements is the most typical representative. It does not seek to reduce things to independent, unchanging atoms, but views the universe as a dynamically balanced whole. The relationships between things (such as mutual generation and restriction, things of the same kind moving each other) received more attention than the absolute properties of the things themselves. This thinking method was good at grasping macro and complex systems (such as traditional Chinese medicine, ecological observation), but felt inadequate when dealing with physical experiments requiring precise isolation of variables.

#### Section 2: The Parallax of the View of Nature: The Germination of Mechanism and Organic Holism
In understanding "nature" itself, China and the West also took different paths.

1. **Western Reductionism and the Undercurrent of Mechanical Universe**
Although the medieval Western view of nature was mainly organic and teleological (influenced by Aristotle and the Church), the atomism of Democritus in ancient Greece was always an undercurrent. Atomism advocated reducing complex macroscopic phenomena to the collision and combination of tiny, featureless atoms. This reductionist thinking laid the seed for the mechanical universe view of Descartes and others in the 17th century (viewing the universe as a giant mechanical clock).

2. **China's Qi Theory and Organic Universe**
Ancient China's view of nature was always organic holism. Under the concept of "Qi," everything in the universe was composed of continuous, flowing "Qi," presenting different forms through the aggregation and dispersion of Qi. Heaven, earth, and man were regarded as a mutually sensing system (unity of heaven and man). This view of nature believed that nature has inherent vitality and self-organization ability, requiring no external "first push" or creator to make laws.

#### Section 3: The Social Construction of Knowledge: Practical Technology and Pure Pursuit of Knowledge
Scientific knowledge never develops in a vacuum; it is deeply restricted by the social and cultural environment.

1. **China: Pragmatism and Official Monopoly**
Ancient China's scientific exploration was highly restricted by practical needs. Calendars were to guide agriculture and display the legitimacy of imperial power; metallurgy and porcelain making were to meet national and ritual needs. This knowledge was often monopolized by official institutions (such as the Imperial Astronomical Bureau) or secretly passed down as family crafts, lacking an academic community for free exchange and public debate. The imperial examination system was centered on Confucian classics, attracting the intelligence of the vast majority of intellectuals to the fields of literature, history, and philosophy, and natural science was regarded as "petty skills and ingenious crafts," with a low status.

#### Section 4: Missed Paradigms: A Deep Analysis of the Needham Question
The Needham Question asks: "Why did modern science not be born in China?" From the perspective of physics, this is actually "Why did the physics paradigm combining experiment and mathematics not be born in China?"

Through the above analysis, we can draw a conclusion: The reason why China's empirical physical knowledge failed to transform into modern science was not because ancient Chinese scientists lacked the ability of observation and experiment (*Mo Jing* and Shen Kuo have proved this ability), but because of the lack of a **mathematical skeleton** and **formal logic network** to link these pieces of empirical knowledge together.

From the perspective of Thomas Kuhn's paradigm theory, ancient China's organic view of nature constituted a highly stable 'Normal Science' paradigm. It was good at solving practical problems in agriculture, astronomical calendar, etc., and continuously improved within its own framework without encountering sufficient 'Anomalies' to disintegrate its own paradigm. The West, in the crisis of the Aristotelian system, triggered a scientific revolution through the work of Copernicus, Galileo, and others, realizing a 'Paradigm Shift' from qualitative natural philosophy to quantitative experimental science. China did not fall behind in development speed, but was always running smoothly on another paradigm track.

The birth of modern physics required the combination of three key elements:
1. Precise and quantitative experimental observation of natural phenomena.
2. Expressing experimental results in mathematical language (especially geometry and calculus).
3. Seeking universally applicable laws of nature and constructing a self-consistent theoretical system.

Ancient China had a powerful first element, but had a paradigm lack in the second and third elements. China missed the modern physics paradigm not because of backwardness in development speed, but because of difference in development direction. China went to the extreme on the road of organicism and empiricism, but this road itself could not spontaneously lead to the mechanism and reductionism of modern physics.

## Conclusion
Before the 17th century, Chinese and Western physics were like two parallel lines extending in different dimensions. China, with its excellent empirical wisdom and organic view of nature, led the world in many practical technologies and discoveries of specific phenomena; while the West, on the legacy of ancient Greek rationalism, constructed a rigorous but rigid scholastic philosophical system. This parallel state was about to be broken. With the explosion of the Western scientific revolution, these two parallel lines would produce a violent intersection, bringing unprecedented shock to China and even the world.

### 【Scientific Thinking and Education Revelations】

From the parallel development of Chinese and Western physics before the 17th century, we can draw the following revelations regarding scientific thinking and modern education:

1. **The Dialectical Unity of Holism and Reductionism**:
   Ancient China's organic holism reminds us that the universe is an interconnected whole. In modern science education, besides cultivating reductionist thinking to disassemble problems (breaking down complex problems into simple parts), emphasis should also be placed on cultivating system thinking, guiding students to look at complex systems (such as ecology, climate, and complex physical systems) from a global and relational perspective.

2. **Equal Emphasis on Empirical Intuition and Logical Construction**:
   *Mo Jing* and *Dream Pool Essays* demonstrated our ancestors' superb empirical observation and experimental intuition, but lacked the support of axiomatic mathematics and formal logic, making these pieces of knowledge fail to transform into systematic scientific theories. This reveals to modern science education that it is necessary to both encourage students to practice and keenly observe physical phenomena in life, and to cultivate their rigorous logical reasoning ability and the ability to use mathematical language to describe natural laws.

3. **Balance between Pure Curiosity and Practical Drive**:
   Ancient China's scientific exploration was mostly driven by practical needs (such as calendar and navigation), lacking the pure intellectual pursuit of the ultimate laws of the universe originating from ancient Greece in the West. Modern education should pay attention to protecting and stimulating students' pure curiosity, encouraging "useless" exploration not aimed at short-term utility, because it is often this deep inquiry into basic laws that can bring disruptive scientific breakthroughs.
