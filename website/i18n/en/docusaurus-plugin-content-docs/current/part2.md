# Part 2: The Pinnacle of Classical Physics and the Shock of "Western Learning Spreading East" (18th–19th Centuries)

> *"If I have seen further it is by standing on the shoulders of Giants."* —— Isaac Newton

![Inverted Image in Ink Wash Style](/img/part2_ink_wash.png)

## World Axis
The 18th and 19th centuries were the golden age of classical physics. Based on Newtonian mechanics, classical mechanics reached its perfect pinnacle and successfully explained the motion of celestial bodies and ground objects. In the 19th century, thermodynamics and electromagnetism were established successively, and the proposal of Maxwell's equations marked the completion of classical electromagnetic theory. These theories not only constructed the grand palace of classical physics but also directly promoted the Industrial Revolution, completely changing human society.

## China Point
During this period, China experienced the transition from the closure of the Kang-Qian prosperous era to the humiliation and awakening of the late Qing Dynasty. In the 18th century, although missionaries brought some Western scientific knowledge, it was mainly limited to the court and a few scholar-bureaucrats, failing to cause social changes. After the Opium War in the 19th century, the "strong ships and sharp cannons" of Western powers shocked the Qing government and also let people of insight see the huge gap between China and the West in science and technology. The Self-Strengthening Movement rose, and institutions like the Translation Office of Jiangnan Arsenal began to translate a large number of Western physics works. Knowledge of physics as an independent discipline began to be systematically introduced into China.

## Coordinate Value
From the perspective of the coordinate system, this period was a time when the development of Chinese and Western physics moved from "parallelism" to "collision" and "intersection." Western classical physics reached its peak, while China was forced to start the process of modernization under external impact. "Western learning spreading East" was not only the introduction of knowledge but also the shock of thinking methods. China moved from the initial "learning the superior techniques of the barbarians to control them" to the full acceptance of the Western scientific system. This process was full of pain and struggle, but it also laid the foundation for the development of modern physics in China.

## Content
### Chapter 1: The Grand Palace of Classical Physics

#### Introduction: The Golden Age of Classical Physics
From the 18th to the 19th century, physics experienced two hundred years of development under the foundation of Newtonian classical mechanics, building a grand and magnificent theoretical edifice. Physics in this period reached an extremely high level of completeness in theory, and through its combination with the Industrial Revolution, completely changed the production methods and living appearance of human society, becoming the cornerstone of modern civilization.

#### I. The Pinnacle of Newtonian Mechanics and the Development of Analytical Mechanics
Newton proposed the laws of motion and the law of universal gravitation in the *Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy* published in 1687, marking the establishment of classical mechanics.

##### 1. The Birth of Analytical Mechanics
After the 18th century, mathematicians introduced calculus tools to push mechanics to the analytical stage. Lagrange parsed mechanics problems through "generalized coordinates" and the "principle of virtual work"; Hamilton proposed the "principle of least action," providing a concise integral form for mechanics laws and laying a framework for later quantum mechanics. Analytical mechanics made classical mechanics reach a perfect state in mathematics.

##### 2. The Victory of Celestial Mechanics
Newtonian mechanics achieved a brilliant victory in astronomy. Laplace used Newtonian mechanics to explain the motion of celestial bodies in the solar system and proposed "Laplacian determinism." The "discovery at the tip of a pen" of Neptune in 1846 pushed the prestige of Newtonian mechanics to its peak.

#### II. The Establishment of Thermodynamics and the Awakening of Energy
Thermodynamics, which rose in the 19th century, focused on energy conversion and efficiency, directly driven by the improvement of the steam engine in the Industrial Revolution.

##### 1. Carnot Cycle and Laws of Thermodynamics
The "Carnot cycle" proposed by Carnot explored the theoretical limit of heat engine efficiency and laid the foundation of thermodynamics. Subsequently, Mayer, Joule, and Helmholtz independently discovered the law of conservation of energy (the first law of thermodynamics), unifying phenomena in different fields under the concept of "energy."

##### 2. Introduction of Entropy and Directionality
To explain the directionality of heat transfer, Clausius and Kelvin proposed the second law of thermodynamics. Clausius introduced the concept of "entropy" to measure the degree of disorder of a system, revealing the directionality of time, which had a profound impact on physics and philosophy.

#### III. The Unification of Electromagnetism and the Essence of Light
Another great achievement of 19th-century physics was the establishment of electromagnetic theory, which perfectly unified electricity, magnetism, and light phenomena.

##### 1. From Electromagnetic Induction to Field Theory
After Oersted discovered the magnetic effect of electric current, Ampere established the foundation of electrodynamics. Faraday discovered the phenomenon of electromagnetic induction and proposed the concept of "field," changing the traditional concept of action at a distance; his invention of the prototype of generators and motors unveiled the electrical era.

##### 2. Maxwell's Equations and Grand Unification
Based on Faraday's work, Maxwell used mathematical skills to propose a complete electromagnetic field theory in 1865. Maxwell's equations summarized all classical electromagnetic phenomena and predicted the existence of electromagnetic waves, pointing out that light is also an electromagnetic wave, completing another grand unification in the history of physics. Hertz subsequently confirmed this prediction by experiment in 1887.

#### IV. The Worldview of Classical Physics and Its Influence
By the end of the 19th century, the edifice of classical physics had been completed, bringing great confidence to mankind in mastering the laws of nature.

##### 1. Mechanical Determinism and the Industrial Revolution
Classical physics gave birth to the mechanical materialist worldview and promoted the spread of rationalism. At the same time, its results were directly transformed into productivity: mechanics and heat were the cornerstones of the first Industrial Revolution, while the development of electromagnetism gave birth to the second Industrial Revolution, letting mankind step into the "Electrical Era."

##### 2. Crisis Behind the Pinnacle
Although Lord Kelvin believed that the edifice of physics was basically completed, the "two clouds" he mentioned still existing in the sky (the Michelson-Morley experiment and the blackbody radiation problem) triggered a physics revolution in the early 20th century, giving birth to relativity and quantum mechanics. But this does not erase the great status of classical physics as the most precise theory of the macroscopic low-speed world.

##### 3. Eastern Perspective: The Missing "China Point" and Coordinate Review
- **China Point**: During the two hundred years when the edifice of Western classical physics was being completed, China was in a period moving from the peak of the Kang-Qian prosperous era to closure and decline. When Western scientists were using calculus to calculate planetary orbits and using experiments to capture electromagnetic waves, China's intellectual circles were still immersed in traditional righteousness and textual research, knowing almost nothing about this scientific revolution.
- **Coordinate Value**: From the perspective of coordinates on the world physics map, the development of China and the West in this period showed a huge "time parallax" and "paradigm gap." The West advanced triumphantly on the world axis, while China was in a state of stagnation or even marginalization on the scientific coordinates. This strong contrast paved the way for the violent collision after the "strong ships and sharp cannons" blasted open the door of the country in the next chapter.

### Chapter 2: Strong Ships and Sharp Cannons and the Input of Science

#### Introduction: From Celestial Empire to Being Forced to Open Eyes to See the World
In 18th-century China, at the peak of the Kang-Qian prosperous era, the policy of closed-door isolation made China miss the scientific revolution and industrial revolution taking place in the West. Although a few Jesuit missionaries like Dai Jinxian served in the court, bringing some Western astronomical and mathematical knowledge, this knowledge was strictly limited to the small royal circle and failed to transform into a force driving social changes.

Entering the 19th century, the afterglow of the prosperous era gradually dissipated, and the Qing Dynasty moved toward decline. At this time, the West, by virtue of the Industrial Revolution driven by classical physics, demonstrated an astonishing expansionary force. The explosion of the Opium War in 1840, where the British blasted open the door of China with "strong ships and sharp cannons," was not only a tragic military defeat but also a direct collision of two civilizations and two levels of science and technology. People of insight after the war began to realize that the strength of the West lay not only in the precision of its weapons but also in the "science of investigating things" (i.e., science) behind them. Faced with the "great changes unseen in thousands of years," China was forced to start the arduous process of learning from the West.

#### Background Projection of the World Axis
While late Qing China was arduously inputting scientific knowledge, the physical axis of the world was undergoing a dramatic change. The West not only consolidated the palace of classical mechanics but also achieved a decisive victory in electromagnetism and thermodynamics. When Chinese scholars were struggling to understand Newtonian mechanics, Maxwell's electromagnetic theory had already predicted the arrival of a new era. This triumphant advance of Western physics turned into a dimension-reducing strike of military and industrial power through strong ships and sharp cannons, constituting a cold background for China being forced to open its eyes to see the world.

#### I. The Rise of Translation Offices and Systematic Input of Physics Works
Driven by the Self-Strengthening Movement, to achieve the goal of "learning the superior techniques of the barbarians to control them," the Qing government and local forces began to set up specialized institutions to translate Western scientific and technological books. This was the beginning of the systematic introduction of Western physics knowledge into China.

##### 1. The Great Cause of the Translation Office of Jiangnan Arsenal
Founded in 1868, the Translation Office of Jiangnan Arsenal was the most important scientific translation institution in modern China. Here, Chinese people of insight collaborated with Western scholars to translate a large number of Western scientific works.

In physics, the most important collaborators were the British John Fryer and the Chinese scholar Xu Shou. Fryer interpreted orally, and Xu Shou wrote it down; they cooperated tacitly. Facing a large number of physical concepts not in Chinese, Xu Shou showed amazing creativity. For example, in translating *Heat as a Mode of Motion* by Tyndall, Xu Shou and Fryer needed to introduce Western cutting-edge physical concepts about molecular motion and the essence of heat into Chinese. Faced with abstract physical quantities like "latent heat" and "specific heat," Xu Shou continued his rigorous translation style, striving for the unification of literal meaning and physical substance. Although Xu Shou's achievements in the naming of chemical elements are more well-known, his and Fryer's determination of basic concepts like force, heat, sound, and light in physics translations laid the foundation for modern Chinese physical vocabulary, directly promoting the evolution from the science of investigating things to modern physics.

Another important translator was Hua Hengfang, who collaborated with the British missionary Fryer to translate *A New Treatise on Ocean Defense*, *Probability*, etc., which leaned toward military and mathematics but also contained a large amount of mechanics knowledge.

##### 2. Li Shanlan and the Translation of *Zhong Xue* (Mechanics)
In the translation of mechanics (called "Zhong Xue" at that time), Li Shanlan made pioneering contributions. Li Shanlan was a famous mathematician in the late Qing Dynasty. He collaborated with the British missionary Alexander Wylie to translate the work of the British physicist William Whewell, translated as *Zhong Xue* (published in 1859).

*Zhong Xue* was the first Chinese work to systematically introduce Newtonian mechanics. In the book, Li Shanlan creatively translated many core mechanics terms, such as "force," "speed," "acceleration," "momentum," "mass," etc. Although some translated names were different from modern ones, he laid a solid terminological foundation for the spread of mechanics knowledge in China. Li Shanlan emphasized in the preface that mechanics is the root of Western science; to understand manufacture, sailing, and firearms, one must first understand mechanics. It is worth noting that when Li Shanlan was arduously introducing 17th-century Newtonian mechanics to China in 1859, the Western physics community was experiencing the unification of electromagnetism (Maxwell proposed Maxwell's equations in the 1860s). Behind this "time parallax" was a profound contrast between two scientific paradigms and mathematical thinking. Li Shanlan faced the Newtonian mechanical worldview, attempting to use the traditional Chinese "Tian Yuan Shu" (a kind of counting-rod algebra) to understand and translate Western mechanics based on calculus. This was an attempt to use discrete, algebraic traditional thinking to dock with continuous, analytical modern mathematics, full of difficulties in paradigm shifting. Meanwhile, Maxwell in the West was jumping out of the framework of mechanical mechanics, using exquisite mathematical physical methods to mathematize Faraday's intuition "lines of force" and create the theory of "field." When Li Shanlan was still "making up lessons" for China on the classical mechanics of mechanical determinism, Maxwell had already started the paradigm leap of physics from "particles" to "fields." This east and west exactly constituted a strange intersection of the twilight of classical physics and the dawn of new physics.

##### 3. From "Gezhi" to "Wuli": Evolution of Concepts
In the 19th century, Chinese people initially used traditional "Gewu Zhizhi" (investigating things to extend knowledge) to refer to Western science, and physics was usually called "Gezhi" or "Zhongxue" (leaning toward mechanics). It was not until the end of the 19th century and the beginning of the 20th century, with the translation and introduction of a large number of Japanese scientific books after the Sino-Japanese War, that the word "Wuli" originating from Japanese gradually replaced "Gezhi" to become the official name of this discipline. This change in vocabulary also reflected the deepening of Chinese people's understanding of physics—from the broad "probing the principles of things" to a specialized, systematic physical science.

#### II. Modern Education and Classroom Dissemination of Physical Knowledge
Besides the translation of books, the establishment of modern education institutions also provided a platform for the dissemination of physics knowledge in China.

##### 1. Science Education in Beijing Tongwen Guan
Founded in 1862, Beijing Tongwen Guan was initially a foreign language school but quickly added science classes. In 1867, Tongwen Guan added the "School of Mathematics," with Li Shanlan serving as the chief instructor, teaching mathematics and astronomy. Subsequently, physics, chemistry, and other courses were opened.

Tongwen Guan hired Western instructors to teach physics and introduced experimental instruments. Although the level of science education at that time was still elementary and strongly opposed by the stubborn faction, it initiated the precedent of systematically teaching physics knowledge in modern Chinese schools.

##### 2. Gezhi Shuyuan and Social Popularization
Besides official schools, some social education institutions with an enlightenment nature also emerged. Founded in Shanghai in 1876, Gezhi Shuyuan was established by Fryer, Xu Shou, and others. The academy regularly held scientific lectures, exhibited scientific instruments, and published *Gezhi Huibian* magazine, which was the first popular science journal in China.

*Gezhi Huibian* published a large amount of common physics knowledge and answered scientific questions raised by readers, playing an important role in popularizing physics knowledge among intellectuals and ordinary citizens.

##### 3. Dispatching Students Abroad: Directly Absorbing Scientific Sources
To learn advanced Western technology more directly, the Qing government, driven by Zeng Guofan, Li Hongzhang, and others, successively dispatched four batches of a total of 120 children to study in the United States between 1872 and 1875 (i.e., "Chinese Educational Mission").

Although this batch of students was recalled early due to political reasons, outstanding engineering and technical talents like Zhan Tianyou still emerged among them. They received systematic modern science education in the United States, including classical physics. Subsequent Westernization institutions like Fuzhou Naval Arsenal also dispatched students to study navy and manufacture in Britain and France, directly contacting the most advanced applications of mechanics and heat in the West.

#### III. Reaction of Chinese Intellectuals: Birth Pangs from Resistance to Awakening
The introduction of Western physics knowledge was by no means a simple knowledge shifting process; it was accompanied by the painful struggle of Chinese intellectuals' fierce ideological struggle and paradigm shift.

##### 1. Resistance of the Stubborn Faction and "Western Crafts" Theory
In the middle of the 19th century, stubborn literati occupying the mainstream of society held an extreme rejection attitude toward Western science. They believed that Western science was nothing more than "petty skills and ingenious crafts" and could not be mentioned in the same breath as the holy and sage way of China. They attacked Tongwen Guan's addition of science classes as "changing China by barbarians," believing that learning Western skills was a great shame.

This attitude reflected the arrogance and prejudice of the traditional scholar-bureaucrat class, as well as fear of the unknown. They degraded scientific knowledge like physics to pure "crafts" (technology), denying its theoretical value and worldview significance.

##### 2. Compromise of the Westernizers (Yangwu Pai): "Chinese Learning for Substance, Western Learning for Application"
Faced with internal and external troubles, Westernizer officials (such as Zeng Guofan, Li Hongzhang, Zuo Zongtang, etc.) realized that they must learn Western technology. To reduce the resistance of the stubborn faction, they proposed the famous slogan "Chinese learning for substance, Western learning for application."

Under this framework, they believed that China's Confucianism and ethics were the root of the country (substance), while Western science and technology and physics were only means to enrich the country and strengthen the army (application). This dualistic view, although playing a role in protecting science dissemination at that time, also restricted Chinese people's full acceptance of scientific spirit and scientific methods. Intellectuals often only paid attention to the application of physics in manufacturing firearms and ships, while ignoring its rigorous logical system and scientific attitude of experimental verification.

##### 3. Awakening of Enlightenment Thinkers: From Crafts to Scientific Spirit
With the deepening of "Western learning spreading East," by the end of the 19th century, a batch of more far-sighted intellectuals began to transcend the limitations of "Chinese learning for substance, Western learning for application." Reformers like Yan Fu, Kang Youwei, and Liang Qichao began to realize that the strength of the West lay not only in technology but also in its academic and ideological aspects.

Yan Fu translated *Evolution and Ethics*, introducing the theory of evolution, and also strongly praised Western induction and experimental science. They began to realize that what physics represented was a brand-new way of understanding the world, based on observation, experiment, and mathematical reasoning, which was essentially different from traditional Chinese scholarship emphasizing textual research and mind-nature comprehension. This awakening buried the spark for the "New Culture Movement" and "May Fourth Movement" at the beginning of the 20th century that completely broke the shackles of traditional thought.

##### 4. Paradigm Collision and Projection of the "Needham Question"
From the perspective of Kuhn's "Paradigm Shift" theory, the "Western learning spreading East" in the late Qing Dynasty was essentially a violent paradigm collision. Traditional Chinese scholarship was based on experience and utility, lacking the mathematized and logicalized experimental paradigm of the West since Newton. Li Shanlan and others translated *Zhong Xue*, attempting to introduce exactly this brand-new "classical mechanics paradigm." However, restricted by the utilitarian attitude of "Chinese learning for substance, Western learning for application," intellectuals at that time paid more attention to its technical applications rather than the underlying scientific worldview. This also responded from the side to the "Needham Question": On soil lacking scientific paradigm soil, relying only on translation and introduction of technology, it is difficult to spontaneously generate a revolution in modern science.

#### Conclusion: Arduous Sowing
In general, the dissemination of physics in China in the 18th and 19th centuries was extremely arduous and slow. It was a kind of "stress" response forced by China under the background of aggression by Western powers. The input content mainly focused on practical technology, lacked systematicness, and was subjected to strong resistance from traditional stubborn forces.

However, it was exactly the translation of books, establishment of schools, running of newspapers, and dispatching of students abroad in this period that sowed the seeds of modern physics in this ancient land of China. The efforts of pioneers like Li Shanlan and Xu Shou broke the confinement of traditional thought and opened the road for modern science in China. It was exactly these early sparks that ultimately converged into the prairie fire of the vigorous development of modern physics in China in the 20th century.

### 【Scientific Thinking and Education Revelations】

The development course of Chinese and Western physics in the 18th–19th centuries demonstrated in this part provides profound revelations for modern science education:

1. **Integrity of Scientific Paradigm**: Science is not only a collection of knowledge but also a complete paradigm composed of theories, mathematical tools, and experimental verification. The failure of "Chinese learning for substance, Western learning for application" in the late Qing Dynasty showed that merely learning the "application" of science while ignoring its "paradigm" (such as logical reasoning and experimental spirit) cannot truly master science.
2. **The Transition from "Translation" to "Verification"**: The translation work of sages like Li Shanlan and Xu Shou was indispensable, but the true rooting of science needs to move from "text translation" to "experimental verification." Modern education should emphasize hands-on experiment and active inquiry, rather than pure knowledge indoctrination.
3. **Terminology Localization in Cross-Cultural Exchange**: Xu Shou's creation of chemical and physical terminology naming methods in translation demonstrated how to adapt culturally while maintaining scientific rigor. This suggests that in scientific communication, the accuracy and localization of terms are equally important.
